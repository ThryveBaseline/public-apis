#### 3. The 1:1.5 derivation and the trailing-drawdown mechanism

First stated 2026-03-28: ("most evaluations are plus 3,000 before they're minus 2,000. And essentially, that's three to two, which is 1.5 risk to reward." — JJ, 3L8xdh3oPm4 ~12:32, L446). Restated 2026-06-22 (aCOgfvL6lK8 ~7:48, L335), 2026-07-11: ("This, in itself, is a risk-benefit ratio of 1 to 1.5. Therefore, if you split your trades in a 1 to 1.5 ratio" — JJ, J8qXxtFFpGg ~7:36, L325), 2026-07-25: ("split your trades in a 1:1.5 ratio and you'll automatically have a higher approval rate just from how the loss limit behaves." — JJ, KN7j6NXXAio ~5:50, L248), 2026-08-06: ("you'll automatically have a higher pass rate simply because of how that dynamic drawdown adjusts after each trade you make." — JJ, S8fSNbPFuLA ~1:49, L100), and latest, 2026-10-04: ("the evaluation has a $3,000 profit target and then a $2,000 max loss. This in and of itself is a 1 to 1.5. So if you separate your trades into a 1 to 1.5, you're automatically going to have a higher pass rate just because of how the draw down trails." — JJ, KHEQ5g55dQ4 ~8:22, L328); ("finding a good risk-to-reward for the evaluation which is is probably just going to be 1 to 1.5 for everybody if it's a minus 2k plus 3k" — JJ, KHEQ5g55dQ4 ~21:53, L741).

The mechanism is that a trailing drawdown never adds room: ("then you still only have $2,000 worth of draw down. At least you're $1,000 in profit, but you didn't make any new draw down to trade with." — JJ, KHEQ5g55dQ4 ~21:47, L738). Hence target size changes the pass rate at constant edge: ("going for a $1,000 of profit, or 1,500 profit, or 300 profit, are all going to have different pass rates because they're fitting into the rules differently as the drawdown trails." — JJ, DEkzU8gqX-o ~1:55, L95); ("You can optimize your take profit and stop loss because they're all going to generate the same EV in a biased environment." — JJ, DEkzU8gqX-o ~4:59, L199); ("the best approach on a prop firm is to find the one that has the highest pass rate specifically, and then execute that as many times as possible." — JJ, DEkzU8gqX-o ~2:07, L102). Small targets fail by arithmetic: ("you win 200, you lose 200. How many days do you think it's going to take you to reach 3K? Right? Even with the draw down trailing, even if the draw down doesn't trail, you still need to win 15 times more than you lose." — JJ, dJdBnSBJlgQ ~15:26, L468); for a 1:10 style ("it's going to take them forever to hit that plus 3,000 or they're going to need three in a row, which with 1:10 is like extremely unlikely." — JJ, aCOgfvL6lK8 ~9:41, L398); ("by reducing it from one thousand to perhaps one hundred, your success rate will worsen by exactly 33%." — JJ, S8fSNbPFuLA ~8:05, L342, medium confidence, surrounding captions garbled). On the point target he fixes exactly 1.5R: ("19 is the one that, at 1.5, generates the most expected value for me in the evaluations and probably for you too, which is why I'm aiming for exactly 1.5 instead of dragging this down and letting it be 1.8, because that's not optimal for a funding firm." — JJ, 74CRg-mID5c ~26:43, L914).

Break-even at 1.5R: ("the break-even point in that would be 40%. But anything above 40% and you're going to make money in funding companies, for sure." — JJ, KN7j6NXXAio ~16:22, L627); ("A strategy with no advantage would be a 40% success rate with a risk-reward ratio of 1.5, which gives zero advantage per trade." — JJ, 2BtwoTCxcbc ~0:16, L40). His own stated edge over it is small: ("The break-even rate on 1.5 R operations is 40%. Therefore, I am 2% above the break-even point" — JJ, 4IGbxmKJ4BU ~0:56, L68); ("50% with 1.5 R is statistically impossible with a sufficiently large sample size" — JJ, 4IGbxmKJ4BU ~1:13, L80). Against that, a session sample: ("Four wins, four losses. That's 50% at 1.5. That's 10% above the break-even point, which is literally printing money out of thin air." — JJ, 74CRg-mID5c ~32:50, L1116). Given rules, one combination is optimal: ("given the set of rules, you must have an optimal risk-reward ratio." — JJ, J8qXxtFFpGg ~4:59, L223); ("Your success rate and your risk-reward ratio are inversely related. So you need to find out which one works on the funding account and test it." — JJ, J8qXxtFFpGg ~5:45, L253).

#### 1. Funded objective and posture

("on your funded account, you have a completely different set of constraints, new set of math, new optimization problem to solve for because you no longer care about your pass rate" — JJ, 0Ua-ffltO0I ~3:00, L166); ("if I'm given a funded account, what is my average payout amount over the lifetime of that account?" — JJ, 0Ua-ffltO0I ~3:09, L172). The objective is cash out, not P&L or survival: ("When you get to the funded account, you want to optimize for your expected cash withdrawal. Nothing else matters. It doesn't matter how much money you make. It doesn't matter how long your account survives. The only thing that matters is how much in payouts can you get from one account." — JJ, sWJa8vRfPb8 ~4:20, L177). Yet survival is the lever: ("So your goal in the funding firm, instead of maximizing profits, should be to reduce your probability of reaching maximum loss." — JJ, J8qXxtFFpGg ~0:51, L61); ("if you stop reaching the maximum loss, your account will eventually experience exponential growth." — JJ, J8qXxtFFpGg ~0:57, L65). ("Zero is permanent. As soon as you lose your account, there is obviously no more profit potential in it." — JJ, 4IGbxmKJ4BU ~3:33, L180).
